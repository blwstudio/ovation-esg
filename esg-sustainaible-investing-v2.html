The short answer

In plain terms

Sustainable investing means putting your money to work in line with your values, without setting aside sound financial planning.

The terminology is used inconsistently across the industry, and a fund’s name doesn’t always match what it holds. What matters is understanding what a fund actually invests in, what it excludes, and whether the approach fits your wider plan.

On labels

ESG, sustainable, responsible, ethical — these terms are used inconsistently. A fund name is never enough on its own.

On returns

Responsible investing doesn’t automatically mean worse (or better) returns. The honest picture is more nuanced than either camp admits.

On impact

Public-market investing can influence companies through stewardship and voting. Direct real-world impact is real but usually indirect.

We offer three portfolio options — Growth, Responsible, and SEER — with honest explanations of what each actually involves.

What do sustainable, responsible, ethical and ESG actually mean?

These terms get used interchangeably. They shouldn’t be — and understanding the difference matters before you choose a fund or ask for advice.

ESG

Considers environmental, social and governance factors in investment decisions. Fundamentally an analytical lens, it is a way of spotting risks and opportunities that conventional financial analysis can miss. Poor environmental practices may mean future regulatory costs. Weak governance often correlates with higher fraud risk.

Responsible investing

Takes sustainability and governance factors into account across the whole investment process, to protect long-term value and in some cases, to achieve specific sustainability objectives.

Sustainable investing

Typically means investing that meets the needs of the present without compromising future generations. A longer-term, outcomes-focused lens.

Ethical investing

Usually starts with values — what you want to avoid, support or scrutinise. More personal, more explicit, and often involving specific exclusions.

The important thing is not the word on the tin. It’s what the fund actually does and understanding that there is no perfect solution.

Is sustainable investing just greenwashing?

Greenwashing happens when a product claims to be better for people or the planet than it really is. In investment, it has been widespread — fund names, marketing language, and impact claims that the underlying portfolio simply can’t support.

The FCA has introduced UK Sustainability Disclosure Requirements (SDR) to address this directly, including an anti-greenwashing rule, naming and marketing restrictions, and four voluntary sustainability labels that funds can apply for if they meet specific criteria.

The four FCA sustainability labels
LabelWhat it means in plain English
Sustainability Focus Focus funds invest in assets that are environmentally and/or socially sustainable. Examples include investing in assets that have transitioned to renewable energy sources or those that provide healthcare or education services.
Sustainability Improvers Improvers funds invest in assets that have the potential to improve environmental and/or social sustainability over time. Examples include investing in assets with credible plans to reduce their emissions or that have made commitments to improve social standards over time – such as human rights or labour practices.
Sustainability Impact Impact funds aim to achieve a pre-defined positive, measurable impact in relation to an environmental and/or social outcome. Examples include investing in assets facilitating the transition through the deployment of new climate technology solutions, new renewable energy generation or social housing development.
Sustainability Mixed Goals Mixed Goals funds invest in line with a combination of 2 or more of the sustainability objectives for the other labels.

These labels bring meaningful clarity but not every fund carries one. Unlabelled funds making sustainability claims must now make specific disclosures about how they plan to deliver on them.

What this means in practice A fund can be entirely legitimate without an FCA label. And a labelled fund still needs scrutiny — what it holds, what it excludes, and how it votes all matter. We look at all of it.

Does sustainable investing mean worse returns?

This is a question we often hear from clients, and it is quite nuanced.

Investment outcomes depend on diversification, cost, asset allocation, methodology and market conditions — and that’s true of any portfolio, not just sustainable ones.

A broad, diversified portfolio tracking an ESG index can behave quite similarly to a conventional equivalent. The performance gap is often smaller than either proponents or sceptics claim.

A more values-led portfolio with stronger exclusions is a different matter. It may invest in a narrower range of companies, look more different from the wider market, and carry higher fund costs. In that case, a small return trade-off is possible in some periods. That should be understood and accepted as part of the decision, not buried in the small print.

Sustainable investing is not the financial sacrifice it’s often painted as. But it’s not a free lunch either. What matters is to understand what returns you need and how those can be delivered within your investment preferences.

Risk note Investments can fall as well as rise. You may get back less than you invested. Past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results. Sustainable, responsible, ethical and ESG criteria vary significantly between funds, providers and data sources.

Can your investments actually make a difference?

The honest answer is that they can contribute, but the route is usually indirect. If you sell shares in a company, the company probably doesn’t know and it is unlikely to change their behaviour. Real-world impact through public markets is harder to evidence and is often reported anecdotally.

That said, there are three genuine ways investors can try to make a difference and they work better in combination than alone.

Use ownership rights

Fund managers can vote at shareholder meetings, engage directly with company boards, collaborate with other investors, and use policy levers to push for change. Slow, but genuinely influential at scale.

Understand sustainability risks

Companies with poor environmental, social or governance practices may face regulatory, reputational or financial risks. Identifying and avoiding those risks is both ethically sound and financially rational.

Reflect your preferences

Aligning your portfolio with your values has real meaning, even where direct impact is hard to prove. It’s part of living consistently — alongside how you spend, give and choose.

Stewardship in action — Sainsbury’s & living wages

An alliance of international investors representing over €7 trillion in assets engaged Sainsbury’s directly on living wage standards across their supply chain. In 2025, Sainsbury’s was assessed as the best-performing company among eight food retailers evaluated by the Platform for Living Wage Financials — rated ‘advanced’, with supply chain disclosure considered best practice. That is what sustained, coordinated investor engagement looks like in practice.

Sustainability risk in action — Vale SA

Two catastrophic dam failures in Brazil (2015 and 2019) caused devastating environmental damage and the deaths of 272 people. Fund managers who had identified poor governance and environmental risk management at Vale SA had reason to avoid or exit the company before those events. For many responsible investment funds, the company remains ineligible for investment today. This is what ESG risk analysis is designed to catch.

Frequently asked questions

Is ESG investing the same as ethical investing?

No. ESG looks at environmental, social and governance factors that may affect risk and return — it’s an analytical framework. Ethical investing usually starts with personal values: what you want to avoid or support. They overlap, but they’re not the same thing. A fund can score well on ESG and still hold companies you’d rather not.

Does sustainable investing mean worse returns?

Not automatically. A broad, diversified responsible portfolio can behave quite similarly to a conventional one. A values-led portfolio with stronger exclusions may look more different from the market and carry higher costs — so a small return trade-off is possible in some periods. The honest answer is that outcomes depend on fund quality, cost, diversification and market conditions, not on whether something wears an ESG badge.

Can my investments make a real-world difference?

They can contribute, but the mechanism is usually indirect. Selling a company’s shares doesn’t change their behaviour. What fund managers can do is vote, engage, collaborate with other investors, and use capital allocation to push for change over time. That influence is real but it’s not the same as directly causing a specific outcome, and anyone who tells you it is should be challenged on the evidence.

Are sustainable funds regulated in the UK?

UK authorised asset managers are now subject to FCA Sustainability Disclosure Requirements including the anti-greenwashing rule, naming and marketing rules, consumer disclosures, and four voluntary sustainability labels. Some funds fall outside scope, including certain overseas funds and some pension arrangements. The regulation is still bedding in, which is why looking beyond the label matters.

What’s the difference between Ovation Responsible and Ovation SEER?

Responsible is sustainability-aware and mainly passive — ESG-related screens and active stewardship, at relatively low cost. SEER goes further: stronger ethical screening, more active fund selection, more explicit values alignment. The trade-off is higher expected fund costs and a more concentrated portfolio. We’ll walk through both before making any recommendation.

Can I avoid fossil fuels entirely?

A portfolio can often materially reduce fossil fuel exposure, but “fossil-fuel free” is a high bar. Definitions, revenue thresholds and fund methodologies vary widely — one fund’s exclusion is another’s “transitioning company.” If this matters strongly to you, the fund documents need careful checking, and we’ll do that with you.

Can I invest ethically and still be properly diversified?

Usually, yes. A well-constructed responsible portfolio can cover a broad spread of geographies and sectors. The stricter the exclusions, the more the portfolio may differ from the wider market — and that concentration has risk implications worth understanding before you commit.
