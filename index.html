The problem with the label

I spend a lot of time explaining this to clients, so let me be direct: “ESG”, “sustainable”, “responsible” — none of these are regulated terms in the way most people assume. Anyone can put them on a fund.

That doesn’t mean they’re all meaningless. It means the label tells you almost nothing without looking underneath it. I’ve seen funds marketed as sustainable that hold oil majors. I’ve seen “ethical” funds where the exclusions are so narrow they barely change the portfolio at all.

“The right question isn’t ‘is this fund ESG?’ — it’s ‘what is it actually doing, and does that match what I care about?’”

The FCA’s Sustainability Disclosure Requirements are beginning to bring structure to this — fund labels will need to mean something specific. But the framework is still bedding in, and the market is moving faster than the regulation.

For now, the methodology document is your friend. Not the fund name. Not the marketing brochure. What does the fund actually exclude? What does it hold? How does it vote? These are the questions worth asking.

Does investing ethically cost you?

This is almost always the first question. And I understand why — the assumption that doing good means accepting less has been repeated so often it’s almost become received wisdom.

It’s not well supported by the evidence.

Long-term research from MSCI, Morningstar, and others consistently suggests that ESG-oriented portfolios have performed comparably to conventional ones — and in some periods, better on a risk-adjusted basis. The logic isn’t hard to follow: companies with strong governance tend to be better run; those with lower environmental exposure face fewer future regulatory shocks; better labour practices correlate with lower staff turnover and fewer reputational crises.

Where it gets more complicated is in thematic funds — clean energy in particular has seen significant volatility. And narrow exclusions can create stretches of underperformance when excluded sectors rally. So it’s not a free pass.

My honest view: the framing of “ethical vs returns” is mostly a false choice. The more interesting question is fund quality and methodology — which matters whether you’re investing ethically or not.

How ethical investing actually works — the different approaches

Not all ethical investing strategies are the same, and some produce very different portfolios. Here’s what the main approaches actually involve:

Exclusionary screening

Removing specific sectors — tobacco, weapons, fossil fuels. The most common approach and the easiest to explain. The catch: definitions of what counts as ‘excluded’ vary enormously between funds.

Most common · Check the detail carefully

Best-in-class

Investing in the highest ESG-rated companies within each sector — including controversial ones. Maintains diversification, but may still hold companies you’d rather avoid.

Broad exposure · Less prescriptive on exclusions

Thematic

Targeting specific areas: clean energy, water, sustainable food systems. Expresses a positive view rather than just excluding the bad. Introduces concentration and often higher short-term volatility.

Purposeful · Higher concentration risk

Impact investing

Seeking measurable positive outcomes alongside financial returns. More common in private markets. The challenge is verification — “impact” is one of the most overused words in the industry.

High intent · Hardest to evidence

Engagement & stewardship

Rather than excluding companies, some fund managers hold them and actively vote or engage to push for change. Less visible to individual investors, but increasingly influential at scale. Often combined with other approaches.

Often used alongside exclusions or best-in-class

Most clients end up with a combination. Exclusions for the things they’re clear about; a broader ESG tilt for the rest; sometimes a thematic element if there’s a particular area they want to lean into. The shape depends on the person.

Questions I get asked a lot

Does ethical investing mean lower returns?

Not as a rule, no. Long-term research suggests ESG-oriented portfolios have performed comparably to conventional ones — and in some cases better on a risk-adjusted basis. The “you have to sacrifice returns” assumption has been repeated so often it feels true. The data is more nuanced. That said, results vary by fund and by market conditions. Some thematic funds have been volatile. The quality of the fund matters enormously — which is true of any investment.

How do I know if a fund is genuinely ethical or just greenwashing?

Look at what it actually holds, not what it says on the tin. Read the methodology document. Find out exactly what is excluded and how strictly. The FCA’s SDR labelling regime is bringing more transparency, but it’s still early. A good financial planner should be able to show you what a fund is really doing — and tell you honestly if it doesn’t match your brief.

My values are quite specific. Can I still invest in a way that reflects them?

Yes, though it takes more thought than picking a fund off a list. Off-the-shelf ESG funds are built for broad audiences — their definitions may not match yours. If your priorities are specific — particular supply chains, animal welfare, community investment — you’ll want to look at actual holdings rather than rely on labels. In some cases a more bespoke approach makes more sense.

Can I be properly diversified and invest ethically?

Generally, yes. A well-constructed ESG portfolio can cover a broad range of geographies and sectors. Where it gets trickier is when exclusions are particularly wide — avoiding all energy exposure, for instance, including companies genuinely transitioning away from fossil fuels. We’d talk through what that means for your overall plan before making any decisions.

What if I’m not sure what I actually care about?

That’s a completely normal place to start. Most clients don’t arrive with a fully formed values brief. The conversation is part of working it out. What matters most to you? What would you find it hard to be invested in? Are there areas you actively want to support? We work through it together. You don’t need to have the answers before we speak.
